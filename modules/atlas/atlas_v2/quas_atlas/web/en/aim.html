Atoms in molecules: Bader theory

Where does one atom end and another begin in a molecule? Bader theory (QTAIM) answers this from the electron density ρ(r) alone — a quantity that can be both calculated and measured by X-ray structure analysis. An atom is the region around a nucleus bounded by a zero-flux surface: no gradient path of ∇ρ crosses it. Here the density is computed right in the browser by the Hartree–Fock method in a Gaussian basis — from the minimal STO-3G up to 6-31G**.

What to understand

  • An atom in a molecule is the region around a nucleus bounded by a zero-flux surface of ∇ρ; the partition is set by the density itself, not by the basis.
  • Critical points (∇ρ = 0) come in four types; bond critical points and bond paths form the molecular graph, and the numbers of points obey n − b + r − c = 1.
  • The sign of ∇²ρ shows where density is concentrated (∇²ρ < 0) and where it is depleted (∇²ρ > 0); together with ρb and Hb it helps tell a covalent bond from a closed-shell interaction.
  • Bader charges are integrals of the density over atomic basins and converge as the basis grows; Mulliken charges depend on how the basis is distributed over the atoms.
  • The electrostatic potential on the ρ = 0.001 surface shows where the charges and dipoles of a neighbour will approach: a σ-hole is a positive region on the extension of a bond (halogen bonding), a π-hole lies over an empty or depleted p orbital (BF₃, CO₂, C₆F₆).
Model limits: HF density; the topology is more robust than the numbers

The HF method (no correlation), basis sets from STO-3G to 6-31G**; numerical integration of the basins.

1. Density, gradient paths and the molecular graph

The ∇ρ trajectories (thin lines) come in from infinity and usually end at the nuclei: in the typical case the attractors, the maxima of the density, sit at the nuclei, and all trajectories that reach an attractor form the basin of an atom. There are exceptions: non-nuclear attractors (for example in some metals and electrides) and artefacts of an approximate density, as for HF in the STO-3G basis (question 5 below). Trajectories that end not at a nucleus but at a bond critical point form the interatomic surface, the boundary between basins (thick lines). From the same critical point a bond path leads to the two nuclei: the line along which the density is higher than along any neighbouring line.

2. Functions in 3D and the electrostatic potential

An isosurface is a surface on which a function has one and the same value. The surface ρ = 0.001 a.u. encloses more than 95 % of the electrons and is taken as the “outer boundary” of the molecule. The molecular electrostatic potential V(r) = Σ ZA/|r − RA| − ∫ρ(r′)/|r − r′| dr′, the interaction energy with a unit positive charge, is mapped onto it. Red regions (V < 0) attract cations and hydrogen-bond donors, blue regions (V > 0) attract anions and lone pairs. A positive region on the extension of a covalent bond of a halogen atom is called a σ-hole (F₂ has one, although fluorine is the most electronegative atom); a positive region above the plane of a π system or above an atom with a vacant p orbital is a π-hole (CO₂, BF₃, F₂CO, C₆F₆). 3. Critical points

At a critical point ∇ρ = 0. The type of the point is set by the signs of the three eigenvalues of the Hessian of ρ (curvatures λ₁ ≤ λ₂ ≤ λ₃): a maximum (3, −3), usually at a nucleus; a bond critical point (3, −1), a minimum along the bond path and a maximum across it; a ring point (3, +1) inside a ring; a cage point (3, +3) inside a polyhedron. The numbers of points always obey the Poincaré–Hopf relation n − b + r − c = 1. The character of a bond is judged from the density at the bond point ρb, the Laplacian ∇²ρb = λ₁ + λ₂ + λ₃, the ellipticity ε = λ₁λ₂ − 1 and the energy density Hb = Gb + Vb.

4. Atomic charges: Bader, Mulliken, Löwdin

The Bader charge of an atom is the nuclear charge minus the number of electrons in its basin, that is, the integral of the density over the atomic volume. Mulliken and Löwdin charges are obtained differently: the density matrix is split among basis functions “assigned” to atoms. So they depend on which functions sit on which atoms. Check your knowledge

Exercises on the page: do the action on the page and choose an answer — it is checked right away.

Choose C₆H₆ (STO-3G). How many bond critical points were found in total?

Bond points

12: six C–C bonds and six C–H bonds. There is also one ring point (3, +1) at the ring center, and the Poincaré–Hopf relation holds: 12 − 12 + 1 − 0 = 1.

Compare ∇²ρ at the bond point in N₂ and in LiF. What does it say about the bonds?

For N₂ ∇²ρb ≈ −2.5 < 0: density is concentrated between the nuclei — a covalent bond. For LiF ∇²ρb ≈ +0.8 > 0, while ρb is small (0.07) and Hb > 0: density is drawn toward the nuclei, an interaction of the closed shells of Li⁺ and F⁻.

C₂H₄: why is the ellipticity of the C=C bond about 0.45, while for C–H bonds it is almost zero?

The π bond adds density above and below the molecular plane, so at the C=C bond point the density is stretched more in one direction across the bond: λ₁ and λ₂ differ noticeably. For single bonds the density cross-section is almost circular, ε ≈ 0; for a triple bond it is circular too, because two π bonds together have cylindrical symmetry.

H₂O, 6-31G**: which charge of the O atom is larger in magnitude — Mulliken or Bader? Why?

Bader: about −1.2 versus −0.67 by Mulliken. The O–H interatomic surface passes close to the H atom (the bond point is 0.2 Å from it), so almost all of the bond density falls into the oxygen basin. Mulliken splits the overlap equally between atoms regardless of their electronegativity.

Choose HF in the STO-3G basis. Why was no bond critical point found?

In the minimal basis the H atom has no density maximum of its own: along the axis the density decreases monotonically from F through H outward. Without an attractor there is no H basin, and the whole electron “belongs” to fluorine (H charge = +1). This is an artifact of a Gaussian basis without a cusp at the nucleus: already in 6-31G the maximum at H appears together with the bond point.

For CO ∇²ρb > 0. Does this mean the bond in CO is ionic?

No. The density at the bond point is large (ρb ≈ 0.48), and Hb ≈ −0.8 is clearly negative, as for covalent bonds. The positive Laplacian arises because the bond point is shifted toward the C atom and falls into a region where the carbon density is depleted. The sign of ∇²ρb is only one of the criteria, and for polar bonds it is read together with ρb and Hb.

Section 2, ESP, F₂: where on the ρ = 0.001 surface is the potential positive? How does this fit with fluorine being the most electronegative element?

On the extension of the F–F bond, at the “cap” of each atom: about +56 kJ/mol, while the belt around the atom is negative (about −13). The electron taking part in the σ bond is concentrated between the nuclei; beyond the nucleus along the axis the density is depleted, and the nucleus is less screened there. The lone pairs lie in the belt. This is a σ-hole; for Cl, Br and I it is much stronger, and halogen bonding R–X···B rests on it.

Compare CO₂ and BF₃. Where are their positive regions, and how does a π-hole differ from a σ-hole?

In CO₂ the belt around carbon is positive (about +150 kJ/mol) and the ends of the molecule (O) are negative (about −67). In BF₃ the maximum, about +185, lies above and below boron, with about −66 on the F atoms. A σ-hole lies on the extension of a σ bond, a π-hole perpendicular to the plane (or axis) of the molecule, where the central atom has an empty or depleted p orbital. Lewis bases approach CO₂ and BF₃ from exactly this side.

C₆H₆ and C₆F₆ (STO-3G): what is the sign of the potential above the centre of the ring? What follows from it?

Above the benzene ring the potential is negative (about −44 kJ/mol, the π cloud), and the H atoms are positive. Hexafluorobenzene is the opposite: about +91 above the ring, negative on the fluorines. The quadrupole moments of the two molecules have opposite signs, so a mixture of C₆H₆ and C₆F₆ forms a crystal in which the rings are stacked face to face, and anions bind to C₆F₆ above the centre of the ring (anion–π interaction).

At the bond point ρb is large and ∇²ρb < 0. Can one claim from this critical point alone that the molecule is energetically stable, i.e. will not fall apart into fragments releasing energy?

It cannot be determined from these data. A critical point describes the density at a given geometry, while stability is a question of energies: the energy of the molecule compared with the fragments, and a minimum on the potential energy surface. Bond paths and bond points also occur in strained and metastable structures.
