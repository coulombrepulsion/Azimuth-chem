Basis Sets

Molecular orbitals are expanded in basis functions centered on the atoms. Slater-type functions e−ζr behave correctly both near the nucleus and far from it, but two-electron integrals over them are hard to compute. Therefore calculations use Gaussian functions e−αr² and their fixed linear combinations, called contractions. Below we use the hydrogen atom, where the exact answer is known: E = −0.5 a.u.

What to understand

  • MOs are expanded in basis functions; the more complete the basis, the lower the energy (variational principle).
  • Gaussians are fast to compute but poorly describe the nuclear cusp and the long-range tail, so they are combined into contracted functions.
  • Polarization functions let orbitals change shape; diffuse functions describe anions and weak interactions.
  • Cost grows quickly with basis size (formally N⁴ for HF; for CCSD(T) at fixed molecule, as nvirt⁴), so choosing a basis is a compromise between accuracy and time.

Comparison: STO-nG vs. energy-optimized Gaussians

STO-nG sets are fitted to reproduce the shape of the Slater-type function as closely as possible (least squares), not to minimize the energy. Therefore, for the same n their energy is higher than that of a variationally optimized set, but the same contraction works for any ζ: the exponents are simply multiplied by ζ².

One Gaussian: the variational principle and the virial theorem

For the trial function g = (2απ)3/4e−αr² the kinetic energy is T = 3α2 and the potential energy is V = −2√2απ. A tight function (large α) gains in attraction to the nucleus but pays in kinetic energy.

How to read basis set names

STO-3G is a minimal basis: one function per atomic orbital (for C: 1s, 2s, 2px,y,z), each a contraction of three Gaussians.

6-31G is split-valence: the core 1s is a contraction of 6 Gaussians; each valence orbital is described by two functions, a contraction of 3 Gaussians and a single Gaussian. Two functions of different width let the orbital “breathe”: contract or expand in the molecule. 3-21G and 6-311G are built the same way (6-311G has three functions per valence orbital).

* or (d) means polarization d functions on heavy atoms, ** or (d,p) adds p functions on H as well: they let the electron cloud shift away from the nucleus, which matters for bonds and angles. + means diffuse functions on heavy atoms (anions, weak interactions).

cc-pVXZ (Dunning; X = D, T, Q for double, triple, quadruple zeta) are built so that the correlation energy converges systematically as X grows; aug- adds one diffuse function of each type. The notation (9s4p1d)/[3s2p1d] means 9 s, 4 p and 1 d primitives contracted into 3 s, 2 p and 1 d functions.

Convention for d functions: 6-31G(d) uses 6 Cartesian ones (x², y², z², xy, xz, yz; one combination is effectively an s function), 6-311G(d,p) and cc-pVXZ use 5 spherical ones; f has 7, g has 9.

Check your knowledge

Exercises on the page: do the action on the page and choose an answer — it is checked right away.

STO-3G gives an error of about 13 kJ/mol for the H atom. What follows from this?

The error is comparable to conformational and hydrogen-bond energies, so a minimal basis is fit only for qualitative estimates. Polarization functions will not help the H atom: the error lies in the shape of the 1s function itself, and it is fixed by adding s Gaussians.

Switch the ψ plot to a logarithmic scale. Where do the Gaussians describe the function worst?

Near the nucleus (the Slater-type function has a cusp, while a Gaussian has zero derivative at the origin) and at large distances: ln ψ of the Slater-type function is the straight line −r, while for a Gaussian it is the parabola −αr², so it decays too fast. The tails matter for anions and weak interactions, which is exactly why diffuse functions are needed.

Why do three energy-optimized Gaussians give −0.49698, while STO-3G gives only −0.49491?

STO-3G is fitted to reproduce the shape of the Slater-type function (least squares), not to minimize the energy. In return it is universal: for any ζ it is enough to multiply the exponents by ζ².

One Gaussian: how does the ratio −VT behave for different α?

It equals 2 only at the energy minimum (the virial theorem holds for the optimal function), not at other α. So −VT = 2 is a handy check that the function parameters are optimized.

Benzene: how many times more expensive is an HF calculation in cc-pVTZ than in 6-31G(d)?

How many times

N = 264 versus 102; by the formal N⁴ law, (264102)⁴ ≈ 45 times. For CCSD(T) the N⁷ law cannot be applied this way: it describes simultaneous growth of the occupied and virtual spaces. Here there are still 21 occupied orbitals and only the virtual space grows (81 → 243), while the leading (T) term costs ~nocc³nvirt⁴: (243/81)⁴ ≈ 80 times.

Why are large calculations run in “direct” mode, recomputing two-electron integrals at every iteration?

The number of integrals grows as N⁴8: for caffeine in cc-pVTZ (560 functions) that is about 1.2·10¹⁰ integrals, roughly 92 GB. Recomputing them is cheaper than storing and reading them from disk.
