Born–Oppenheimer and vibrational levels

In the Born–Oppenheimer approximation the electrons adjust instantly to the positions of the nuclei, and the nuclei feel a potential curve U(R) — the same for all isotopes. On this curve the nuclei move as quantum particles: they have discrete vibrational levels and zero-point vibrations. The model is the hydrogen molecule with a Morse potential fitted to the experimental De, Re and ωe.

What to understand

  • Nuclei are thousands of times heavier than electrons, so the electronic problem is solved at fixed nuclei, giving the PES U(R).
  • Mass alone is not enough: the approximation breaks down where electronic states come close and nonadiabatic couplings are large (avoided and conical intersections).
  • U(R) does not depend on nuclear masses: isotopologues differ only in their vibrational levels.
  • Even in the ground state the molecule vibrates: zero-point energy makes D₀ differ from De.
  • Levels of a real (anharmonic) well converge toward the dissociation limit.

2. Why the nuclei can be “frozen”

The main motivation for the approximation is that nuclei are heavy: their vibrations are slower than electronic motion, and the zero-point amplitude is small compared with the bond length. Both are set by the mass ratio meμ: the vibrational period 2πω grows as μ1/2, and the amplitude falls as μ−1/4. Move the mass down — for “nuclei” as light as an electron there is no separation any more. But a large mass is not a sufficient condition: the electronic states must also be well separated in energy. Where states come close (an avoided or conical intersection), nonadiabatic couplings mix them, and the approximation fails even for heavy nuclei — see the avoided crossing on the PES page.

How does the zero-point energy change when H₂ is replaced by D₂?

Decreases by about √2: ω ∝ 1√μ, and the reduced mass of D₂ is twice as large. For H₂ E₀ ≈ 26 kJ/mol, for D₂ about 18.4 kJ/mol.

Which molecule has the larger dissociation energy D₀ — H₂ or D₂?

D₂ (4.56 eV versus 4.48 eV): the well is the same but the zero-point vibrations are smaller, so the level from which D₀ is measured lies lower.

Why do H₂ and D₂ have the same De but different D₀?

In the Born–Oppenheimer approximation U(R) is set only by the electrons and the nuclear charges; nuclear masses do not enter the electronic problem. The masses affect only the nuclear motion: the frequency and zero-point vibrations, and hence D₀ = De − E₀.

How does the number of bound vibrational levels change from H₂ to D₂?

Increases by about √2 (17 levels for H₂ and 25 for D₂ in the Morse model; the real molecules have 15 and 22): the levels of the heavier molecule are denser, while the well depth is the same.

How does the spacing between neighboring vibrational levels change as v increases?

It decreases: for Morse ΔEv→v+1 = ωe − 2ωexe(v + 1), and the levels crowd toward the dissociation limit. For the harmonic oscillator the spacing is constant and equals ωe.

Decrease the mass μ with the slider. Why does the Born–Oppenheimer approximation stop working for very light “nuclei”?

The zero-point energy becomes comparable to the well depth, and for μ ≲ 7 me fewer than two levels remain in it: the “nucleus” moves as fast as the electrons, and the motions can no longer be separated.
