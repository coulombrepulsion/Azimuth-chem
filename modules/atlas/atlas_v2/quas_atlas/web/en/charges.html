Atomic charges: comparing schemes

Programs print “atomic charges”, but the charge of an atom in a molecule cannot be measured: it results from a convention on how to divide the electron density between atoms. Different schemes divide it differently and give different numbers for the same wavefunction. Here Mulliken, Löwdin and Bader charges are computed from the same Hartree–Fock calculation in four basis sets — you can see which conclusions drawn from charges are reliable and which are not.

What to understand

  • The charge of an atom in a molecule is not observable: it results from the chosen way of dividing the electron density between atoms.
  • Mulliken and Löwdin divide the density by basis functions; Bader divides space itself, so his charges converge as the basis grows. In small basis sets they also change noticeably: from STO-3G to 6-31G** the Bader charge of oxygen in water changes by 0.4 e.
  • Comparing charges makes sense only within one scheme and one basis set: in a series of similar molecules, trends are more reliable than absolute numbers.
  • Observables — the dipole moment and the electrostatic potential — test how well the charges represent the electrostatics of the molecule.
Model limits: charges are a convention, not an observable
What is computed
Mulliken, Löwdin and Bader atomic charges for the same RHF wavefunction in four basis sets; the dipole moment of point charges compared with the real one.
Which approximations are made
Hartree–Fock without correlation; basis sets from STO-3G to 6-31G**; the NBO, Hirshfeld and ESP-fitting schemes are described in words but not computed. Löwdin charges in basis sets with six Cartesian d functions (6-31G*, 6-31G**) are computed after orthonormalising each such shell, so they do not depend on the orientation of the molecule (with the direct definition the equivalent H atoms of NH₃ got 0.2677 / 0.2692 / 0.2692).
What can be concluded
How strongly a charge depends on the scheme and basis set and which comparisons make sense — yes; the “true” charge of an atom — no: no such quantity exists.

As the basis grows, the energy and dipole moment converge to a limit; Mulliken and Löwdin charges may change non-monotonically — there is no limit they should converge to.

Check with the dipole moment

The dipole moment is an observable: it can be measured and computed from the full electron density. Placing only the point charges of a scheme on the nuclei gives the “dipole moment of the charges” μq = Σ qARA. The closer μq is to the real value, the better the charges represent the electrostatics of the molecule.

How the schemes work

Mulliken
The population of an atom is the diagonal elements (PS)μμ of its basis functions; the overlap density is split equally between the two atoms. Simple and fast, but strongly basis-dependent, especially with diffuse functions.
Löwdin
The basis is first orthogonalized symmetrically (S1/2PS1/2), then the populations are computed. There is no overlap left to divide; the basis dependence is smaller but remains.
Bader (QTAIM)
Space is divided by zero-flux surfaces of the density gradient; the charge is the integral of the density over the atomic basin. The scheme is defined by the density itself rather than by the basis and converges with the basis; the charges are usually larger in magnitude.
Other schemes
NBO (natural orbitals), Hirshfeld (the share of the free-atom density), charges fitted to the electrostatic potential (CHELPG, RESP — fitted to the ESP and used in force fields). For comparisons, use one scheme and one basis set.

Check your knowledge

Exercises on the page: do the action on the page and choose an answer — it is checked right away.

Water, 6-31G**: how much do the Mulliken and Bader charges of oxygen differ for the same wavefunction?

Difference in O charge, e

By 0.55: −0.67 by Mulliken and −1.23 by Bader (−0.42 by Löwdin). The wavefunction is the same; only the rule for dividing the density between atoms differs.

LiH in STO-3G: what is the sign of lithium in the Mulliken scheme? What should you believe?

Almost zero and even slightly negative (−0.02) — contrary to chemistry: lithium donates an electron to hydrogen, as seen both from the dipole moment (about 4.9 D) and from the Bader charge (+0.84). Mulliken splits the overlap density equally and in a minimal basis can produce such artifacts.

Water: add p functions on hydrogen (6-31G* → 6-31G**). How did the Mulliken charge of oxygen change, and why?

From −0.87 to −0.67: the new functions are centered on hydrogen, and by the Mulliken rule part of the O–H bond density is now assigned to hydrogen. The energy changed by only 0.013 Eh and the dipole moment by 0.04 D: it is not the molecule that changes but the bookkeeping.

Methane, 6-31G**: what is the sign of the carbon charge in the Bader and Mulliken schemes?

By Bader carbon is positive (+0.24), by Mulliken negative (−0.47). Both schemes are correct by their own definitions; the conclusion “carbon in methane carries a negative charge” depends on the scheme and therefore says nothing about the molecule.

CO, 6-31G*: does the direction of the Hartree–Fock dipole moment agree with experiment?

No. In experiment μ = 0.11 D with the negative end on carbon, while Hartree–Fock in 6-31G* gives C⁺O⁻. The correct sign is obtained only with correlation. In STO-3G the sign is right, but by accident.

Water, 6-31G**: what dipole moment do the Bader point charges give (in debye)? Why is it larger than the real one?

Bader μq, D

About 3.45 D versus 2.19 D from the full density (experiment 1.85 D). The atomic basins are also polarized: their own dipole moments partly cancel the charge transfer, and point charges do not account for this.
