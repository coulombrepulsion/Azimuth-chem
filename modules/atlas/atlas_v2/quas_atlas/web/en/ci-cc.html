Configuration interaction and coupled clusters

The exact wavefunction in a given basis is a combination of all determinants that can be built from the orbitals: this is full CI. Their number grows so fast that an exact calculation is feasible only for tiny systems. Here you can see why truncated CI (CISD) unexpectedly spoils the energy of large systems and how the exponential form of the coupled-cluster method fixes it. The H₂ model in a minimal basis is solved right on the page; the (H₂)n series in the 6-31G basis was computed with PySCF.

What to understand

  • Full CI is the exact solution in a given basis, but the number of determinants grows factorially: even in STO-3G benzene has about 3·10¹⁹ of them.
  • CISD includes only single and double excitations and is therefore not size-consistent: for n non-interacting molecules its correlation energy grows only as √n.
  • In the coupled-cluster method Ψ = exp(T)·Φ₀; the exponential itself generates products of amplitudes (½T₂² and so on), so CCSD is size-consistent.
  • CISD and CCSD cost the same (N⁶); the (T) correction adds triple excitations by perturbation theory at N⁷.

What can be concluded
Why truncated CI is not size-consistent and how the exponential ansatz fixes it — yes; the accuracy of the methods for real molecules — no: that is shown on the “Hierarchy of methods” and “Basis set convergence” pages.

How many determinants are there

A determinant is obtained by choosing which spin orbitals are occupied. An excitation of level k replaces k occupied spin orbitals by k empty ones. Full CI includes all levels, CISD only single and double excitations.

H₂ in a minimal basis: two configurations

From the two orbitals σg and σu two configurations fit the ground state: σg² (Hartree–Fock) and σu². The 2 × 2 CI matrix couples them through the exchange integral K = (σgσu|σgσu). For two electrons CISD here coincides with full CI.

Size consistency: n molecules that do not see each other

The energy of n non-interacting molecules must be exactly n times the energy of one. Full CI and coupled clusters satisfy this, CISD does not: the total wavefunction has no configurations in which two molecules are excited at once (that is already a quadruple excitation).

Model

The exponential ansatz

In the coupled-cluster method Ψ = eTΦ0, where T = T₁ + T₂ + … are excitation operators. For two distant molecules T = TA + TB, and eTA+TB = eTA·eTB: the wavefunction factorizes into a product by itself. The ½T₂² term gives simultaneous excitations of both molecules, although T contains none.

Why CISD “loses” energy
In CISD n molecules have only configurations with one excited molecule. Their contribution to the second-order correction grows as n, and so does the normalization of the function; as a result the correlation energy grows only as √n, and less and less of it falls on each molecule.
Coupled clusters
CCSD has the same parameters as CISD (the amplitudes t₁, t₂), but the exponential adds their products “for free”: disconnected quadruple, sextuple excitations. So the method is size-consistent, and for two-electron fragments CCSD is exact.
Cost
CISD and CCSD cost the same — N⁶, but the CCSD equations are nonlinear and are solved iteratively. The (T) correction for triple excitations costs N⁷ and gives the “gold standard” CCSD(T).
Where CC breaks down too
Coupled clusters are built from a single determinant. If several configurations matter at once (bond breaking, biradicals), the amplitudes become large and CCSD(T) drops below the exact energy — see the page “Hierarchy of methods and Jacob’s ladder”.

Water in the 6-31G* basis with spherical d functions (18 orbitals, 10 electrons): how many determinants are there in full CI? Answer in millions.

Determinants, millions

C(18, 5)² = 8568² ≈ 73.4 million: five α electrons and five β electrons are distributed independently over 18 orbitals. With six Cartesian d functions (the convention of the “Basis sets” page) there are 19 orbitals and ≈ 135 million determinants. In cc-pVDZ (24 orbitals) it is already 1.8 billion; with symmetry and a frozen core it is fewer, but the growth is of the same order.

H₂ in STO-3G, R = 0.75 Å: what is the weight (in percent) of the σu² configuration in the CI function?

Weight of σu², %

About 1.3 %: near equilibrium Hartree–Fock is almost exact in the composition of the function, but the correlation energy is already 21 mEₕ (13 kcal/mol).

Stretch the bond to 3 Å. What is the weight of σu² now?

Weight of σu², %

About 46 %: the configurations are almost equally important. At full dissociation their weights become equal, and the function describes one electron on each atom. A single configuration σg² is already qualitatively wrong here.

The “H₂/STO-3G” model in the section on size consistency, R = 0.75 Å: what share of the correlation energy does CISD recover for 10 non-interacting molecules, %?

Share of correlation, %

About 90 %: for one molecule CISD is exact, for two it gives 98.7 %, for ten 90 %, for a hundred 57 %. The CISD correlation energy grows as √n, while it should grow as n.

Why is CCSD size-consistent while CISD is not?

For distant fragments T = T_A + T_B, and e^(T_A+T_B) = e^(T_A)·e^(T_B): the wavefunction factorizes into a product by itself. Products of amplitudes give simultaneous excitations of both fragments without new parameters, while the linear CISD function does not contain them.
