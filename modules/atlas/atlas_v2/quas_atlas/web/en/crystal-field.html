Crystal field and ligand field

In a complex the five d orbitals of the metal are no longer degenerate: orbitals pointing at the ligands go up, the others go down. How the levels split is decided by the symmetry of the surroundings. The energies here come from the angular overlap model (AOM): each ligand raises a d orbital by eσFσ² + eπFπ², where F is the σ- and π-type overlap of the orbital with the ligand. The parameters eσ and eπ are illustrative values for M³⁺ ions of the 3d series.

What to understand

  • Ligands lift the degeneracy of the metal d orbitals; the splitting pattern is set by the symmetry of the surroundings.
  • In an octahedron eg lies above t2g by Δo; Δo depends on the ligand (spectrochemical series) and on the metal.
  • The spin state is decided by the competition between the splitting Δ and the pairing energy P.
  • Uneven occupation of a degenerate level distorts the complex (Jahn–Teller effect).

2. High or low spin

For octahedral d⁴–d⁷ complexes two configurations are possible. High spin is favored when the splitting Δo is smaller than the pairing energy P, low spin when it is larger. The lines show the d-electron energy of both configurations versus Δo; the vertical bar marks the chosen ligand.

3. The Jahn–Teller effect

If a degenerate level in an octahedron is unevenly occupied, a distortion lifts the degeneracy and lowers the electron energy — linearly in the size of the distortion. The elastic energy grows quadratically, so the minimum lies at a nonzero distortion. The effect is strongest for uneven occupation of eg: d⁹ (Cu²⁺), high-spin d⁴ (Cr²⁺, Mn³⁺), low-spin d⁷.

4. Symmetry: level labels and correlation

The level labels are the irreducible representations of the point group by which the d orbitals transform (the quadratic-function column of the character table). On descent in symmetry Oh → D4h the degenerate levels split: eg → a1g + b1g, t2g → b2g + eg.

What is the CFSE of low-spin [Fe(CN)₆]⁴⁻ in units of Δo?

CFSE, Δₒ

Fe²⁺ is d⁶, low spin: t2g⁶eg⁰, CFSE = 6 · (−0.4Δo) = −2.4Δo (without the pairing energy). All electrons are paired — the complex is diamagnetic.

How many unpaired electrons does [Fe(H₂O)₆]²⁺ have?

Unpaired electrons

Four: H₂O is a weak-field ligand, Δo < P, configuration t2g⁴eg² (high spin). Select d⁶ and H₂O, then CN⁻ — you will see the switch to low spin.

Switch to the tetrahedron. Why are tetrahedral complexes almost always high-spin?

The splitting Δt ≈ 49Δo: there are four ligands instead of six, and no d orbital points straight at a ligand. Δt is almost always smaller than the pairing energy P.

Which geometry makes a d⁸ complex with strong ligands diamagnetic?

Square planar: dx²−y² rises very high, and all eight electrons pair up in the four lower orbitals — as in [Ni(CN)₄]²⁻ and [PtCl₄]²⁻ (Pt(II), a 5d metal, has a much larger splitting than this model's 3d parameters give: here with Cl⁻ the electrons pair only for P below ≈ 12 500 cm⁻¹). In an octahedron d⁸ always has two unpaired electrons in eg.

Why do CO and CN⁻ give the largest splitting?

They are π acceptors (eπ < 0 in the model): their empty π* orbitals lower t2g, and Δo = 3eσ − 4eπ grows. Halides are π donors (eπ > 0); they raise t2g and reduce Δo.

What does the eg level of the octahedron become on descent in symmetry Oh → D4h?

eg → a1g (dz²) + b1g (dx²−y²), and t2g → eg (dxz, dyz) + b2g (dxy). This can be seen in the correlation table of section 4 and in the labels on the diagram with the distortion switched on.
