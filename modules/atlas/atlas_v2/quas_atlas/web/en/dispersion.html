Intermolecular interactions: hydrogen bond and dispersion

Two helium atoms attract each other although they have neither a charge nor a dipole moment, and their electron shells are closed. This is the dispersion interaction: instantaneous fluctuations of the electron density of one atom induce correlated fluctuations in the other. The Hartree–Fock method cannot describe this correlation, since each electron in it moves in a mean field. Dispersion therefore lies entirely in the correlation energy. This page compares two systems: the helium dimer, held together by dispersion alone, and the water dimer, where the electrostatics of the hydrogen bond plays the main role. All curves are computed with the same HF and MP2 program in a Gaussian basis as on the Bader theory page.

What to understand

  • The interaction energy is a small difference of large energies: ΔE = E(AB) − E(A) − E(B).
  • Dispersion is a correlation effect: HF has none, and He₂ is unbound in HF; at long range it decays as −C₆/R⁶.
  • The main part of the hydrogen-bond energy (electrostatics, polarisation, charge transfer) is described already by HF, but the share of correlation depends on the basis: in this calculation it is about 2 kJ/mol, in the complete-basis limit about a quarter of the binding energy.
  • Basis set superposition error creates spurious attraction; the counterpoise correction removes it, otherwise one can get the right answer for the wrong reason.
Model limits: HF and MP2 in small basis sets
What is computed
Interaction curves of He₂ and (H₂O)₂, the counterpoise correction, the R−6 law.
Which approximations are made
MP2 instead of an exact method; the aug-cc-pVDZ and 6-31G* basis sets; rigid monomers; the energies are computed in advance. The 6-31G* basis here has Cartesian d functions, as in the calculations of the Atlas (the “Basis set superposition error” page uses spherical ones). The water-dimer reference 21.0 kJ/mol (5.02 kcal/mol) is a different estimate from the −4.86 kcal/mol on that page.
What can be concluded
Qualitatively: dispersion is a correlation effect, and BSSE distorts the result. Quantitatively the answer depends on the basis.

1. Interaction energy: HF and MP2

ΔE(R) = E(AB) − E(A) − E(B)

The interaction energy is a small difference of large numbers: the total energy of the water dimer is about −152 Eh, while ΔE is about −0.008 Eh. So even small systematic errors matter here. The best known is the basis set superposition error (BSSE): in the dimer each monomer “borrows” the basis functions of its neighbour, lowers its own energy and creates spurious attraction. The counterpoise correction (Boys and Bernardi) computes E(A) and E(B) in the full dimer basis: only the partner functions remain at its position, without nuclei or electrons (“ghost atoms”).

2. Dispersion decays as R−6

At long range the dispersion energy of He₂ tends to −C₆/R⁶ (the London asymptote; for helium C₆ = 1.46 a.u.). On log–log axes a power law becomes a straight line with slope −6. Shown here is the counterpoise-corrected MP2 correlation contribution: ΔE(MP2) − ΔE(HF). It hardly depends on density overlap and so shows dispersion most cleanly.

Check your knowledge

Exercises on the page: do the action on the page and choose an answer — it is checked right away.

He₂, counterpoise-corrected HF: is there a minimum on the curve? Why?

No: the curve is repulsive at all R. Dispersion is the correlated motion of electrons on different atoms, that is, correlation; in HF each electron moves in a mean field, and there is no such correlation. The small dip of uncorrected HF (about −0.3 cm⁻¹) is an artifact of the superposition error.

He₂: what fraction of the exact well depth (7.65 cm⁻¹) does counterpoise-corrected MP2 give?

About a third: −2.78 cm⁻¹ at 6.2 bohr. The aug-cc-pVDZ basis is too small for dispersion — diffuse functions with high angular momenta are needed, and MP2 itself describes dispersion only approximately.

Turn off the counterpoise correction: MP2 gives an almost exact He₂ well depth. Is this a good result?

No. The agreement is accidental: the superposition error (about 5 cm⁻¹ at the minimum) compensates for the basis deficiency. In a larger basis the BSSE shrinks and the “good” result disappears — this is the right answer for the wrong reason.

Section 2: how does the dispersion energy decay with distance, and why?

As R⁻⁶: on log–log axes the slope is ≈ −6. An instantaneous dipole on one atom induces a dipole on the other, and the energy of a dipole interacting with an induced dipole is proportional to R⁻⁶ (the London formula).

(H₂O)₂, 6-31G* basis: what part of the binding energy of the dimer does this calculation already get at the HF level?

In this calculation almost all of it: corrected HF gives about −19.9 kJ/mol, MP2 about −21.8. Electrostatics, polarisation and charge transfer are described by the mean field, and correlation adds about 2 kJ/mol here. But this is a result of the small basis: HF in the complete-basis limit gives about −15 kJ/mol against an exact energy of about −21, so roughly a quarter of the binding energy comes from correlation. The “2 kJ/mol” must not be generalised to hydrogen bonds as such.

(H₂O)₂: why is the superposition error larger in MP2 than in HF?

The correlation energy converges with the basis much more slowly than the HF energy, so a monomer gains more from the neighbour functions. In the 6-31G* basis the BSSE is about 8 kJ/mol for MP2 and about 4 for HF.
