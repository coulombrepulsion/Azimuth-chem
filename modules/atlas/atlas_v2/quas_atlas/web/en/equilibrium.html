Equilibrium: from energy to constant

The equilibrium constant is set by the standard Gibbs energy of reaction: K = exp(−ΔG°/RT). A calculation gives the electronic energies of the participants; zero-point vibrations and thermal corrections are added to them (see the “Thermochemistry from frequencies” page) to obtain ΔH°, ΔS°, ΔG° and K at any temperature. Here you can see the contribution of each step and how sensitive K is to an error in the energy.

What to understand

  • The equilibrium constant is set by the standard Gibbs energy of reaction: K = exp(−ΔG°/RT); it is computed from the electronic energies of the participants plus zero-point vibrations and thermal corrections.
  • Zero-point vibrations noticeably change the energy of reactions in which bonds to hydrogen are formed or broken.
  • The sign of ΔH° determines how K depends on temperature; the sign of ΔS° determines which way temperature shifts ΔG°.
  • An error of 5.7 kJ/mol in ΔG° at 298 K changes K tenfold: even “chemical accuracy” gives K only to within an order of magnitude.
Model limits: ideal gas, standard pressure 1 bar
What is computed
ΔH°, ΔS°, ΔG° and the equilibrium constant of a gas-phase reaction at any temperature — from the energies and frequencies of the participants.
Which approximations are made
Thermal corrections use the ideal-gas, rigid-rotor, harmonic-oscillator model; the electronic reaction energy is back-calculated from the tabulated ΔH°(298); ideal gas mixture.
What can be concluded
How ΔG° and K change with temperature and how an energy error turns into an error in K — yes; the exact constant for a real process (pressure, non-ideality, solvent) — no.

Check your knowledge

Exercises on the page: do the action on the page and choose an answer — it is checked right away.

Ammonia synthesis at 298 K: which step makes ΔH° half as large in magnitude as the electronic reaction energy?

Zero-point vibrations: ΔEel = −161 kJ/mol, ΔZPE = +84 kJ/mol, ΔH°(0) = −77. Two NH₃ molecules contain six N–H bonds with large zero-point energies, while N₂ and 3H₂ have four bonds. Without ZPE the reaction energy is off by a factor of two.

At what temperature does the equilibrium constant of ammonia synthesis become equal to 1 (ΔG° = 0)?

Temperature at which K = 1, K

About 457 K. The reaction is exothermic (ΔH° < 0) and reduces the number of moles of gas (ΔS° ≈ −198 J/(mol·K)), so on heating −TΔS° grows and the equilibrium shifts to the left. The Haber process is run at 700–800 K for the sake of rate, and the loss of yield is compensated by pressure.

Water–gas shift (CO with steam): find K at 1000 K. Why does K change so slowly with temperature?

K at 1000 K

K(1000 K) ≈ 1.4. ΔH° is only −41 kJ/mol and the number of moles of gas does not change, so ΔS° is small (−42 J/(mol·K)): the slope of lg K versus 1/T is proportional to ΔH°, and with a small ΔH° the constant changes slowly.

Steam reforming of methane: why is it run at 1100–1200 K?

The reaction is strongly endothermic (ΔH° ≈ +206 kJ/mol), but ΔS° ≈ +214 J/(mol·K): two gas molecules become four. ΔG° passes through zero at about 895 K; above it the equilibrium lies on the product side; at 1000 K, K ≈ 24.

“Sensitivity” section: an error of 4 kJ/mol in ΔG° at 298 K — by what factor will K be wrong?

Factor by which K is wrong

By a factor of 5.0: exp(4000/(8.314·298.15)) = 5.0. A tenfold change in K corresponds to 5.7 kJ/mol. The “chemical accuracy” of a calculation gives the equilibrium constant to within a factor, not to within percent.
