The helium atom and electron correlation

Two electrons and a nucleus: the simplest system in which correlation matters. The exact energy of He is known (−2.903724 a.u.), so one can see how much each approximation recovers. By definition, the correlation energy is the difference between the exact energy and the Hartree–Fock limit: Ecorr = Eexact − EHF.

What to understand

  • Electron repulsion makes the helium atom impossible to solve exactly; the simplest approximation is an effective charge (screening).
  • Hartree–Fock accounts for repulsion only on average; the remaining energy error is the correlation energy.
  • Correlation is the concerted avoidance of electrons (the Coulomb hole); it is described by functions that depend explicitly on r₁₂.
  • Sometimes correlation matters qualitatively: without it the H⁻ ion is unstable.
Model limits: variational functions; the exact energy is known
What is computed
The energy of He and He-like ions for functions from one parameter to Hylleraas functions; the Coulomb hole.
Which approximations are made
A non-relativistic Hamiltonian, an infinitely heavy nucleus; a finite number of terms in the function.
What can be concluded
Variational energies computed with the full Hamiltonian are upper bounds to the exact one; the row without electron repulsion solves a different problem and is not covered by this rule. The 6-term Hylleraas function differs from the exact value by less than 0.001 Eh.

1. The ladder of approximations

System

Hylleraas (1929): the trial function e−ζ(r₁+r₂)·(1 + c₁r₁₂ + …) depends explicitly on the interelectronic distance r₁₂. The coefficients are found by the linear variational method, and ζ is optimized. The HF and exact values are tabulated.

2. Effective nuclear charge: screening

The simplest trial function is a product of two 1s orbitals with charge ζ in place of Z: ψ = ζ³π e−ζ(r₁+r₂). Then T = ζ², Vne = −2Zζ, Vee = 5ζ8. Each electron partly shields the nucleus from the other.

3. The Coulomb hole

Fix electron 1 at distance r₁ from the nucleus (on the z axis) and look at where electron 2 is. In the product function (no correlation) electron 2 “knows nothing” about the first one: its distribution is the same for any position of electron 1. In the Hylleraas function electron 2 moves away from electron 1, and a Coulomb hole forms around it.

Section 2: find the optimal effective nuclear charge for He. What does it mean?

Optimal ζ

ζ = Z − 516 = 1.6875, E = −2.8477. Each electron shields the nucleus from the other by 516 of a charge; at the minimum VT = −2.

Why is the energy “without repulsion” (−4) below the exact value (−2.9037), although the variational principle forbids going lower?

The variational principle refers to the full Hamiltonian. Here the electron repulsion is dropped, so a different problem is solved and the estimate is not variational.

What fraction of the He correlation energy does the Hylleraas function with one r₁₂ term give, and why?

About 70% of −0.0420 a.u. (≈ −110 kJ/mol), and 97% with three terms. The r₁₂ term directly describes electrons keeping farther apart.

Select H⁻. Which approximations predict a stable ion?

Only the correlated ones. The effective charge (−0.473) and even the HF limit (−0.488) lie above −0.5 (H + e⁻): the extra electron is not bound. Already the function with an r₁₂ term gives −0.509 < −0.5. The stability of H⁻ is a pure correlation effect.

Compare Ecorr for H⁻, He, Li⁺, Be²⁺.

−0.040; −0.042; −0.044; −0.044 a.u. Nearly constant, although the total energy changes from −0.53 to −13.7: the correlation energy of an electron pair in one orbital is ≈ 1 eV.

The Coulomb hole: how does correlation change the probability of finding the second electron on the same side of the nucleus?

It decreases: at r₁ = 0.6 bohr about 45 % instead of 50 %. ⟨r₁₂⟩ grows from 1.362 to 1.422 bohr. By itself this does not yet prove that the repulsion is smaller: the repulsion energy equals ⟨1/r₁₂⟩, not 1/⟨r₁₂⟩. It has to be computed separately: ⟨1/r₁₂⟩ decreases from 1.0258 to 0.9460 Eh (exact value 0.9458). The reference is the Hartree–Fock function, the best function without correlation, so the difference of the curves is the Coulomb hole proper.
