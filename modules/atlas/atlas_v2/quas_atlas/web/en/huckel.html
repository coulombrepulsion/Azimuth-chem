Hückel method

π-electron systems of planar conjugated molecules: each atom contributes one p orbital, with Coulomb integral α and resonance integral β (β < 0) between neighbors; overlap is neglected. Choose a molecule or build your own; clicking an atom changes its type (C → N → O), clicking a level shows the orbital.

What to understand

  • In the Hückel method the π system is defined only by atom connectivity: α on atoms, β between neighbors.
  • MO coefficients give charges, bond orders, the delocalization energy and reactivity indices.
  • The 4n + 2 rule follows from filling the levels on the Frost circle.
  • Charges (charge control) and frontier orbitals (orbital control) can point to different reactive sites.
  • Electronegativity χ = (I + A)/2 and hardness η = (I − A)/2 are derivatives of the energy with respect to the number of electrons; the Fukui functions f⁺ and f⁻ show where the molecule accepts or gives an electron — in the Hückel model these are the LUMO and HOMO densities.

Heteroatom parameters

αX = α + hβ, βCX = kβ (Streitwieser values). A heteroatom with h > 0 is more electronegative than carbon: its level is lower and the electron density on it is higher. An atom that donates a lone pair to the system (pyrrole-type N, furan-type O) contributes 2 π electrons.

Global indices and Fukui functions

The energy as a function of the number of electrons E(N) is approximated by a parabola through E(N − 1), E(N), E(N + 1), i.e. through the ionisation energy I and the electron affinity A. Its slope is the chemical potential μ = −χ, its curvature the hardness η.

From Hückel to xtb: semi-empirical methods

The Hückel method is the first step of a ladder of approximate methods. Extended Hückel takes all valence orbitals and the overlap; CNDO, INDO and NDDO (MNDO, AM1, PM3, PM7) introduce two-electron integrals but neglect the differential overlap of different atoms; GFN2-xTB is tight binding with charges, multipoles and D4 dispersion for the whole table up to Rn. Check your knowledge

Exercises on the page: do the action on the page and choose an answer — it is checked right away.

Butadiene: compare the π bond orders 1–2 and 2–3. What does this say about the bond lengths?

π bond order 2–3

p₁₂ = 0.894, p₂₃ = 0.447: the central bond is partly double. Estimated lengths are 1.356 and 1.437 Å: the terminal bonds are longer than in ethylene, the central one shorter than a single bond. Delocalization energy 0.472β.

Allyl: switch the charge between +1, 0, −1. What changes in the levels, and what in the atomic charges?

The levels do not change (α + 1.414β, α, α − 1.414β); only the occupation of the nonbonding MO changes. It has a node on the central atom (coefficients ±0.707 at the ends), so the charge of +0.5 in the cation and −0.5 in the anion sits only on the terminal atoms.

Benzene and cyclobutadiene: compare the delocalization energies and the level filling on the Frost circle.

Benzene: 6 = 4n + 2 electrons, all bonding MOs are filled, DE = 2β. Cyclobutadiene: 4 = 4n, two electrons occupy the degenerate pair at α, DE = 0, and in Hückel theory it is a biradical (antiaromaticity).

Cyclopentadienyl: which charge makes the system aromatic? Compare the cation and the anion.

Anion: 6 π electrons, closed shell, DE = 2.472β — hence the high acidity of cyclopentadiene. Cation: 4 electrons, the degenerate pair is half-filled, two unpaired electrons.

Naphthalene: where will an electrophile go, and where a radical? Which bond is the shortest?

The α positions (1, 4, 5, 8): HOMO density 0.181 versus 0.069 at β, free valence 0.453 versus 0.404. The shortest bond is 1–2 (p = 0.725, ≈ 1.387 Å).

Azulene is an isomer of naphthalene. Look at the molecular diagram: where does its dipole moment come from?

The five-membered ring is negatively charged (−0.173 on C1 and C3), the seven-membered ring positively (+0.145 on C4 and C8, +0.130 on C6): like a combination of the cyclopentadienyl anion and the tropylium cation. An electrophile attacks C1/C3, a nucleophile attacks the seven-membered ring.

Acrolein, nucleophilic attack: compare the “frontier orbitals” and “π charges” criteria.

By the LUMO — atom C3 (c² = 0.431), i.e. 1,4-addition, typical of soft nucleophiles. By the charges — the carbonyl C1 (q = +0.333), i.e. 1,2-addition of hard nucleophiles. The two criteria describe orbital and charge control of the reaction.

Select benzene and click atom 1 to make it a pyridine-type nitrogen. How did the charges change, and where will a nucleophile go now?

−0.195 on nitrogen, +0.077 on C2/C6, +0.050 on C4, almost zero on C3/C5. A nucleophile goes to positions 2 and 4, an electrophile (by charges) to position 3: exactly how pyridine behaves.

“Global indices”: which atom of the table is the most electronegative by Mulliken, and what is its hardness?

Fluorine: χ = (17.42 + 3.40)/2 = 10.41 eV, η = (17.42 − 3.40)/2 = 7.01 eV — the most electronegative and one of the hardest. Chlorine is less electronegative and softer (η = 4.68 eV).

Fukui functions of acrolein CH₂=CH–CH=O: which atom will a nucleophile attack by the largest f⁺?

Cβ: f⁺ = 0.43 against 0.33 on the carbonyl carbon. This is conjugate (Michael) addition; soft nucleophiles attack Cβ, hard ones the carbonyl carbon (charge control).
