Reaction rate: transition state

Transition state theory links the rate constant to the Gibbs energy of activation — the energy difference between the transition state and the reactants: k = (kBT/h)·exp(−ΔG‡/RT). The transition state is found by calculation as a saddle point with one imaginary frequency. Here you can see which barriers give reactions over seconds, hours and years, and where the isotope effect and the tunneling correction come from.

What to understand

  • In transition state theory k = (kBT/h)·exp(−ΔG‡/RT): the rate is set by the Gibbs energy of the transition state relative to the reactants.
  • At 298 K every 5.7 kJ/mol of barrier changes the rate tenfold; a barrier of about 90–100 kJ/mol means minutes to hours.
  • The entropy of activation matters as much as the enthalpy: ordered transition states slow the reaction down.
  • The isotope effect and tunneling are consequences of the quantum nature of nuclei: zero-point vibrations and barrier penetration.
Model limits: transition state theory
What is computed
The rate constant and half-life from ΔH‡ and ΔS‡; the isotope effect from the zero-point vibration of a C–H bond; the Wigner tunneling correction.
Which approximations are made
The Eyring equation with a transmission coefficient of 1; a single stretching mode in the isotope effect; the Wigner correction is valid only when κ is close to 1.
What can be concluded
Orders of magnitude of rates, the role of ΔS‡, the sensitivity of k to barrier errors — yes; an accurate rate constant requires an accurate barrier and treatment of tunneling and dynamics.

Barrier and reaction time

The time scale is logarithmic. At 298 K every 5.7 kJ/mol of barrier slows the reaction tenfold.

Kinetic isotope effect

If a C–H bond breaks in the transition state, its stretching vibration ceases to be a vibration — and its zero-point energy is lost. The zero-point energy of C–D is smaller, so the barrier for deuterium is higher. The simplest estimate is kH/kD = exp[hc(νH − νD)/(2kBT)].

Tunneling: the Wigner correction

Light particles (an H atom, a proton) can pass through the barrier. The simplest correction uses the imaginary frequency of the transition state: κ = 1 + (1/24)(hcν‡/kBT)². It is valid only while κ is close to one.

Exercises on the page: do the action on the page and choose an answer — it is checked right away.

A unimolecular reaction at 298 K, ΔS‡ = 0. Adjust ΔH‡ so that the half-life is about an hour. What is the barrier?

ΔH‡, kJ/mol

About 94 kJ/mol (22.5 kcal/mol). A useful guide: at room temperature a barrier of 80 kJ/mol means seconds, 95 about an hour, 110 weeks.

A calculation is off by 4 kJ/mol in the barrier. By what factor will the rate constant be wrong at 298 K?

Factor by which k is wrong

By a factor of 5.0: exp(4000/RT). A tenfold change corresponds to 5.7 kJ/mol. That is why even accurate methods predict absolute rates only to within an order of magnitude, while relative rates (between similar reactions) are better, thanks to error cancellation.

Compare ΔS‡ = 0 and ΔS‡ = −50 J/(mol·K) at the same ΔH‡. How many times slower is the second reaction?

Times slower

exp(50/8.314) ≈ 410 times slower — the same as raising the barrier by 15 kJ/mol at 298 K. A negative entropy of activation is typical of bimolecular and cyclic transition states (for example, the Diels–Alder reaction).

The isotope effect for a C–H bond with a frequency of 2900 cm⁻¹: what is it at 298 K, and why does it decrease on heating?

kH/kD at 298 K

kH/kD ≈ 6.4 at 298 K and ≈ 3.0 at 500 K. The effect is exp(ΔZPE/RT): the difference in zero-point energies stays constant while RT grows, so the ratio tends to 1.

An imaginary frequency of 1500i cm⁻¹ at 298 K: what is the Wigner correction, and can it be trusted?

κ

κ ≈ 3.2. The Wigner formula is the first term of an expansion and holds only for κ close to 1; for κ > 2 tunneling must be treated with the Eckart model or semiclassically. A large imaginary frequency (a narrow barrier) is a sign of H-atom transfer, where tunneling matters.

“Your own reaction” block: for a bimolecular reaction in solution, the energies come from a gas-phase calculation at 1 bar. Why must ΔG‡ be converted to 1 mol/L?

A rate constant in L/(mol·s) refers to the 1 mol/L standard state, while the energies in the output file refer to the gas at 1 bar. Converting each participant to 1 mol/L raises its G by RT ln 24.8 = 7.96 kJ/mol at 298 K; a bimolecular reaction has one more reactant molecule, so ΔG‡ decreases by 7.96 kJ/mol and k grows by a factor of about 25.
