Hierarchy of methods and Jacob’s ladder

Which method should be chosen for a calculation? Wavefunction methods form a hierarchy: each next level is more accurate and more expensive than the previous one, and at the top is full CI, the exact solution in the given basis. Perdew arranged density functionals on “Jacob’s ladder”: the rungs differ in what the functional knows about the electrons. All numbers on this page were computed: errors of methods against full CI as a bond is stretched, the run time for water clusters, and atomization energies of nine molecules.

What to understand

  • Wavefunction methods form the hierarchy HF → MP2 → CCSD → CCSD(T) → full CI: each level is closer to the exact answer in the given basis and more expensive.
  • The hierarchy is reliable as long as one configuration dominates; when a bond breaks, methods built on a single determinant, CCSD(T) included, break down.
  • The cost grows as a power of the system size: N⁵ for MP2, N⁶ for CCSD, N⁷ for CCSD(T), and factorially for full CI.
  • Jacob’s ladder ranks functionals by their ingredients: the density, its gradient, the kinetic energy density, exact exchange, virtual orbitals; higher is usually more accurate, but not always.

How to choose

First, the physics of the problem
If the molecule has one dominant configuration (an ordinary closed shell near equilibrium), the hierarchy works: CCSD(T) in a good basis is the “gold standard”. When bonds break, for biradicals and for many transition-metal complexes, single-determinant methods break down — multiconfigurational methods are needed.
Then, the size
CCSD(T) is practical up to tens of atoms, MP2 up to one or two hundred, DFT up to thousands. For a large system a hybrid functional with a dispersion correction is usually the best compromise.
A functional’s rung is not a guarantee
Jacob’s ladder describes ingredients, not accuracy: a well-fitted GGA is sometimes better than a poor hybrid. A functional is tested on problems of the same type as yours.
Method and basis go together
An expensive method in a small basis makes no sense: the basis set error outweighs the gain from the method. Basis set convergence is on the next page.

The HF molecule at the equilibrium distance: how many times closer to full CI is CCSD(T) than MP2?

How many times

About 9 times: the MP2 error is 3.7 mEₕ, the CCSD(T) error 0.4 mEₕ. With one dominant configuration each level of the hierarchy is noticeably more accurate than the previous one.

Stretch the HF bond threefold. What happened to CCSD(T)?

The CCSD(T) energy fell 17 mEₕ below full CI. The method is not variational, and with nearly degenerate configurations the (T) correction diverges: the answer looks “better than exact”, but this is a breakdown of the method.

The system has doubled. How many times longer does a CCSD(T) calculation take?

Times longer

About 2⁷ = 128 times (the formal N⁷ estimate). That is why CCSD(T) with a large basis remains a method for small molecules.
