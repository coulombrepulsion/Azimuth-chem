Machine learning in quantum chemistry

Machine learning in quantum chemistry is not only potentials for dynamics. A model learns the correction of a cheap method to an expensive one, predicts properties from a structure and picks candidates out of databases of hundreds of thousands of compounds. Here you see why a correction is easier to learn, how to test a model honestly and how stable compounds are selected.

What to understand

  • Δ-learning: Eexpensive = Echeap + Δ. The correction varies over a set less than the energy itself, and with the same number of data its error is smaller. The cheap calculation is always done — the model only adds the correction.
  • A learning curve δ = A·n−a is a straight line in log axes. To halve the error, 21/a times more data are needed: three times for a = 0.65.
  • Kernel ridge regression: f(x) = Σαjk(x, xj), (K + λI)α = y. The solution costs n³: instant for a thousand molecules, not for a hundred thousand — there neural networks are used.
  • The MAE on a test set is a mean, its uncertainty σ(|e|)/√n ≈ 0.75·MAE/√n: ±17 % on twenty molecules. Test molecules must not get into the training.
  • Screening: a compound is stable when its formation energy is below the convex hull of the known phases. Models err by 30–50 meV/atom, so candidates near the hull are checked with DFT.

Why is the correction Δ to a cheap method easier to learn than the energy itself?

The error of a model at a given number of data is proportional to the spread of what it learns. The energy varies a lot, the correction little and smoothly. This is why the Δ model is more accurate, above all when expensive calculations are few.

A learning curve δ = A·n−0.65. By how much must the training set grow to halve the error?

21/0.65 = 2.9 times. With a = 0.41 (as for the Δ model on the silicon of the lecture) 5.4 times: Δ gives a head start, not a fast growth.

The MAE of a model on 20 test molecules is 10 kJ/mol. Within what is it known?

±, kJ/mol

≈ 1.7: σ(|e|) ≈ 0.75·MAE, δ = 0.75·10/√20 = 1.7. Models with MAE 10 and 11 cannot be told apart on such a test.

AB₂ (x = 1/3): −0.40, AB (x = 1/2): −0.50 eV/atom. A new compound at x = 0.4 has −0.41 eV/atom. Where is it?

The hull at x = 0.4: −0.40 + (0.4 − 1/3)/(1/2 − 1/3)·(−0.10) = −0.44. The compound is 30 meV/atom above — within the error of the model: such a candidate is worth checking with DFT.
