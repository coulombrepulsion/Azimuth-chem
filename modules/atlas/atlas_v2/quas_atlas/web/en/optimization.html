Geometry optimization

Almost every quantum chemical calculation begins with a search for the equilibrium geometry — a minimum on the potential energy surface. The program knows the energy and its gradient at the current point and moves step by step toward a point where the gradient is zero. Here the problem is solved on a real surface: the energy of the H₂O molecule computed by HF/6-31G* on a grid of bond lengths r and angles θ (both O–H bonds equal, C2v symmetry). Compare how many steps different algorithms need.

What to understand

  • Optimization looks for a point where the energy gradient is zero; whether it is a minimum or a saddle is decided by the Hessian.
  • Steepest descent is slow in elongated valleys: the stable step length is limited by the stiffest mode.
  • The Newton method converges in a few steps but needs the Hessian; the quasi-Newton BFGS method builds it from gradients — the standard in programs.
  • After optimization frequencies are computed: all real — a minimum, one imaginary — a transition state.
Model limits: HF/6-31G* for H₂O, symmetric structures only
What is computed
The paths of four optimisation algorithms and a 2 × 2 Hessian on a grid of H₂O energies.
Which approximations are made
The HF method without correlation, the 6-31G* basis; the energy is interpolated on a grid; the two O–H bonds are equal.
What can be concluded
The comparison of algorithms is reliable; the minimum is proven only on the map, and the third mode is checked on the “Checking the calculations” page.

2. What was found: the minimum and frequencies

The gradient is zero at a saddle point too, so the point found is checked with the matrix of second derivatives (the Hessian). At a minimum all its eigenvalues are positive. From the Hessian and the atomic masses one gets the harmonic vibrational frequencies — the “frequency calculation” that follows every optimization.

Check your knowledge

Exercises on the page: do the action on the page and choose an answer — it is checked right away.

Steepest descent, α = 0.5: why does the path first go almost across the valley and then creep slowly along it?

The gradient is perpendicular to the contours, and they are elongated: the curvature along r (≈ 1.2 Eh/bohr²) is about 6 times that along θ (≈ 0.19 Eh/rad²). So the bond length is corrected quickly and the angle slowly.

Increase α for steepest descent. What happens at α ≈ 1.6–2?

The step exceeds 2/λmax ≈ 1.65: along the stiff direction (bond stretching) each step overshoots the minimum farther and farther, and the path swings back and forth. The stable step length is limited by the stiffest mode.

Compare descent with line search and BFGS in the table. Why is line search wasteful, even though it takes few steps?

Each step needs dozens of energy evaluations to find the minimum along the direction, and in a real calculation each evaluation is a full SCF calculation. BFGS takes few steps and wastes almost no extra energies.

Newton–Raphson converges in 3–4 steps. Why do programs use BFGS by default?

A Newton step needs the exact Hessian — the second derivatives of the energy, which cost as much as dozens of gradients. BFGS builds an approximate Hessian from gradients already computed and is almost as good in the number of steps.

How do you check that the point found is a minimum and not a transition state?

By the Hessian: at a minimum all its eigenvalues are positive, that is, all frequencies are real. At a transition state one eigenvalue is negative — one imaginary frequency.

Why are the HF/6-31G* harmonic frequencies of H₂O about 10 % too high?

Without correlation the HF surface is steeper than the real one, and the bond is shorter. In addition, observed frequencies are lower than harmonic ones because of anharmonicity. So HF frequencies are multiplied by a scaling factor of ≈ 0.9.

The algorithm has converged, and both eigenvalues of the Hessian on the map are positive. Can one claim that the lowest minimum of H₂O has been found?

It cannot be determined from these data. The map tests only symmetric structures, so the antisymmetric vibration must be checked first (the “Checking the calculations” page does this). And even a true minimum may be local: the local Hessian says nothing about other minima. H₂O has no other minimum, but that is knowledge about the molecule, not a conclusion from the calculation.
