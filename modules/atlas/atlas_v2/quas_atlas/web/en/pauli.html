Pauli principle and the exchange hole

Two electrons in a one-dimensional box, each in its own orbital φn(x) = √2·sin(nπx). Left: map of the probability of finding the first electron at x₁ and the second at x₂. Right: where the second electron ends up if the first is fixed at x₁ (move x₁ on the map or with the slider).

What to understand

  • The electronic wave function is antisymmetric under exchange: Ψ(1, 2) = −Ψ(2, 1).
  • Hence the Pauli exclusion principle: two electrons with the same spin cannot occupy one orbital.
  • Electrons with the same spin avoid each other even without repulsion — this is the exchange (Fermi) hole.
  • Hence the triplet usually lies below the singlet of the same configuration (Hund rule); the difference is set by the exchange integral K.

Electron repulsion energy: J and K

J is the Coulomb integral: the repulsion between the clouds φa² and φb². K is the exchange integral; it has no classical analog and arises from antisymmetry. The repulsion here is one-dimensional and smoothed, so the numbers themselves are arbitrary; what matters is how they relate to each other.

Coulomb hole: configuration mixing

The singlet of two electrons in φ₁ is a single determinant |φ₁φ̄₁|, as in Hartree–Fock. Its electrons have opposite spins, and this function contains no correlation of their instantaneous positions: there is no Coulomb hole. The average Coulomb repulsion is still included — it enters the mean field and shapes the orbitals. Now add a second configuration:

Check your knowledge

Exercises on the page: do the action on the page and choose an answer — it is checked right away.

Select “Triplet” and set φa = φb. What do you see, and why?

Ψ ≡ 0: the spatial part of the triplet is antisymmetric and vanishes identically when the orbitals are the same. Two electrons with the same spin in one orbital — forbidden by the Pauli principle.

Triplet with MS = 0: the electrons have different spins. Is there a dip at x₂ = x₁?

Yes, c(x₁) = 0. The dip is determined by the antisymmetric spatial part, which is the same for all three triplet components.

For φ₁ and φ₂ compare the repulsion energies of the singlet and the triplet. Why is the triplet lower?

The triplet is lower by 2K: with equal spins the spatial function is antisymmetric, the electrons avoid each other (exchange hole), and the repulsion is smaller. This is the basis of the Hund rule.

Turn on “signed Ψ” and press P₁₂ for the triplet and for the singlet. How do the results differ?

For the triplet all the colors are reversed: P₁₂Ψ = −Ψ. For the singlet the picture does not change: the spatial part is symmetric, and antisymmetry comes from the spin factor.

CI block: press “Energy minimum” at λ = 3 and at λ = 8. How do c₂ and the density dip change?

c₂ ≈ −0.27 and a dip of ≈ 68 % at λ = 3; c₂ ≈ −0.52 and a dip of ≈ 99 % at λ = 8. The stronger the repulsion, the greater the weight of the second configuration and the deeper the Coulomb hole.
