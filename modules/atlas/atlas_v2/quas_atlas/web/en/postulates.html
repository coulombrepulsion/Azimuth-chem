Postulates of quantum mechanics: state, evolution, measurement

A particle in a one-dimensional box of length L is the simplest system that shows all the postulates. The eigenfunctions of the Hamiltonian are ψn(x) = √2L sin nπxL, with energies En = n²E₁. Build a state from them, start the time evolution and make measurements.

What to understand

  • A state is described by a wave function; |Ψ|² is the probability density.
  • Stationary states do not change in time (apart from an overall phase), while superpositions of states with different energies evolve.
  • A measurement gives one of the eigenvalues with probability |cn|² and puts the system into the corresponding state; ⟨E⟩ is the average over many measurements.
  • Position and momentum cannot both be sharply defined: Δx·Δp ≥ ħ2.

The five postulates of quantum mechanics

1. StateEverything that can be known about the system is contained in the wavefunction Ψ. Here Ψ = Σ cnψn, set by the amplitudes and phases cn.→ section 1
2. Observables are operatorsEnergy corresponds to Ĥ, position to x̂, momentum to p̂ = −iħ ddx. The possible measurement results are the eigenvalues of the operator.→ section 4
3. ProbabilitiesThe probability of obtaining En is |cn|². The probability density of finding the particle at x is |Ψ(x)|².→ sections 2, 3, 5
4. Measurement changes the stateAfter a measurement the system is in the eigenstate with the value obtained — repeating the measurement gives the same result.→ sections 2, 3
5. EvolutionBetween measurements Ψ changes according to the Schrödinger equation: each ψn acquires the phase e−iEnt/ħ.→ section 1

2. Measuring the energy

The state set in section 1 is the initial one: this is how the system is prepared before the experiment. A single measurement gives one of the values En at random and changes the state: afterwards the system is in ψn, and a repeated measurement gives the same result. So the probabilities |cn|² cannot be seen on one system — you need a series: many systems in the same initial state, each measured once. The “Reset” button undoes the effect of the measurement and returns the system to its initial state.

3. Measuring the position

The device finds the particle with precision σ. A simple “measure and prepare a packet” idealization is used here: the point x₀ is drawn from the original |Ψ|², and the state is replaced by a real Gaussian packet of width σ around x₀, discarding the phase of the original function. In a more general finite-resolution measurement model the original Ψ is multiplied by a Gaussian around x₀ and renormalized, and the statistics of readings account for the device resolution. A narrow packet is a superposition of many ψn, so the energy becomes highly uncertain and the packet spreads in time.

5. Superposition and the expectation value

The state of section 1 is a superposition Ψ = Σ cnψn. Each energy measurement gives one of the En, and the mean of many measurements tends to the expectation value ⟨E⟩ = Σ |cn|² En — the sum of the contributions of the separate states. The deviation of the mean from ⟨E⟩ falls as ΔE√N.

Exercises on the page: do the action on the page and choose an answer — it is checked right away.

Choose ψ₂ and start the time evolution. Does |Ψ|² change? Why?

No. For a stationary state the factor e−iE₂t/ħ is common to the whole function: Re Ψ oscillates, while |Ψ|² and all averages stay constant.

The state ψ₁ + ψ₂. With what period does ⟨x⟩ oscillate (in units of T₁)?

Period of ⟨x⟩, T₁

T₁3: the beat frequency is E₂ − E₁h = 3E₁h, and T₁ = hE₁. The larger the energy difference, the faster the density changes.

Prepare ψ₁ + ψ₂ + ψ₃ with equal amplitudes. What is ⟨E⟩ in units of E₁?

⟨E⟩, E₁

⟨E⟩ = 1 + 4 + 93 = 143 ≈ 4.67 E₁. This is the average over many measurements; a single measurement gives only 1, 4 or 9 — never 4.67.

Press “Single measurement” twice in a row without resetting the state. What does the second measurement give?

The same value: after the first measurement the system is in the eigenstate ψn (postulate 4), and the probability of any other outcome is zero.

Compare Δx·Δp for ψ₁ and for the packet after a position measurement with σ = 0.02 L.

For ψ₁ it is 0.57ħ, for the narrow Gaussian packet almost exactly ħ2: a real Gaussian packet is close to the minimum allowed by the uncertainty relation. This is a property of the chosen measurement model, not of every position measurement. But Δp of the packet is huge — hence the wide spread of energies.
