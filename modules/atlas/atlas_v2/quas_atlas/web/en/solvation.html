Solvent models

Most reactions take place in solution, while a quantum chemical calculation by default describes a molecule in vacuum. The cheapest way to account for the solvent is to replace it with a continuum of dielectric constant ε: the molecule sits in a cavity, polarizes the medium, and the medium responds with a reaction field. Here are the simplest Born model for ions and the polarizable continuum (C-PCM) for neutral molecules, computed with PySCF and compared with experimental hydration free energies.

What to understand

  • An implicit model replaces the solvent with a continuum of dielectric constant ε; the molecule polarizes the medium, and the reaction field polarizes the molecule.
  • The Born model gives the ion solvation energy −694.7·z²/a·(1 − 1/ε) kJ/mol; the key parameter is the effective cavity radius.
  • PCM describes the electrostatics of polar molecules, but without cavity and dispersion terms it is wrong for nonpolar ones.
  • Beyond ε ≈ 20 the solvation energy hardly changes: what matters most is whether the solvent is polar or not.
Model limits: continuum: electrostatics only
What is computed
Ion hydration energies from the Born model; the C-PCM electrostatic solvation energy of eleven molecules (B3LYP/6-31+G*, ε = 78.36) and its dependence on ε for water and acetone.
Which approximations are made
No cavity, dispersion or repulsion terms; the HF/6-31G* geometries are not relaxed in solution; cavity radii are the PySCF defaults; the standard state is 1 mol/L in both phases. C-PCM scales the polarization field as (ε−1)/ε by construction, so the similarity of its ε dependence to the Born formula is partly built into the method.
What can be concluded
How a solvent stabilizes polar molecules and ions and why a continuum is not enough — yes; solvation energies to chemical accuracy — no: that needs models with non-electrostatic terms (for example SMD) and explicit solvent molecules.

The Born model: an ion in a dielectric

A charged sphere of radius a moved from vacuum into a medium of dielectric constant ε gains the energy ΔG = −(NAq²/8πε0a)·(1 − 1/ε) = −694.7·z²/a[Å]·(1 − 1/ε) kJ/mol. The main question is what the radius is: crystal ionic radii give too large a gain.

The polarizable continuum for molecules (C-PCM)

The cavity is built from spheres around the atoms; polarization charges appear on its surface, enter the Hamiltonian of the molecule and are recomputed in every self-consistent-field cycle. Here B3LYP/6-31+G*, water (ε = 78.36), HF/6-31G* geometries. ΔG is the electrostatic contribution only.

How the answer depends on ε

The same calculation in solvents of different polarity. The electrostatic solvation energy is roughly proportional to the factor (ε − 1)/ε: it grows fast at small ε and almost stops changing beyond ε ≈ 20.

What a continuum lacks

Non-electrostatic terms
Creating a cavity in water costs energy, and dispersion and repulsion with the solvent molecules partly return it. Models such as SMD add these terms through empirical formulas fitted to experiment; without them nonpolar molecules come out too “hydrophilic”.
Specific interactions
A continuum describes hydrogen bonds with the first water shell only on average. For anions and molecules with strong hydrogen-bond donors a few water molecules are added explicitly, and this cluster is then embedded in the medium (the cluster–continuum model).
Cavity radii
The result depends noticeably on the radii of the atomic spheres: they are the main fitted parameter. That is why solvation models are compared and used together with the radii they were calibrated for.
Standard states
Here the transfers compared are “1 mol/L in the gas → 1 mol/L in solution”. If the gas is taken at 1 bar, RT·ln(24.79) = 7.96 kJ/mol (1.90 kcal/mol) is added — a common source of discrepancies when computing pKₐ values and equilibrium constants in solution.

The Born model, ε = 78.4, zero radius increments: what hydration energy does it give for Na⁺ (kJ/mol)?

ΔG(Na⁺), kJ/mol

ΔG = −694.7/1.02 · (1 − 1/78.4) ≈ −672 kJ/mol — almost twice the experimental value (−365) in magnitude. The crystal radius is too small for a cavity in a continuum.

Choose the increment δ₊ for which the Born model gives −365 kJ/mol for Na⁺.

δ₊, Å

a = 694.7·(1 − 1/78.4)/365 ≈ 1.88 Å, i.e. δ₊ ≈ 0.86 Å — almost the same increment of 0.85 Å that Latimer, Pitzer and Slansky proposed for all cations in 1939.

C-PCM for water: by how many kcal/mol does the calculation differ from experiment (calculation minus experiment)?

Calculation − experiment, kcal/mol

About −1.3 kcal/mol: −7.6 versus −6.3. Electrostatics without the cavity term overestimates the gain, and for a molecule that forms hydrogen bonds the continuum describes the first hydration shell only on average.

Why does C-PCM give a negative solvation energy for ethane while the experimental value is positive (+1.8 kcal/mol)?

The electrostatic term is always negative: the medium only stabilizes the charge distribution. The positive part — the cost of forming a cavity in water — is not computed by this model, and for a nonpolar molecule it is exactly the main one.

The section on the dependence on ε, acetone: what share of its solvation energy in water does it already get in chloroform (ε = 4.8), %?

Share, %

About 77 %: the factor (ε − 1)/ε is 0.79 at ε = 4.8 and 0.99 in water. So a calculation “in some polar solvent” differs little from one in water, but going from the gas to chloroform already gives the main part of the effect.
