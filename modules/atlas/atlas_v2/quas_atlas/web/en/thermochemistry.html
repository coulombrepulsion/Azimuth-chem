Thermochemistry from frequencies

A quantum chemical calculation gives the electronic energy for fixed nuclei. To obtain the enthalpy, entropy and Gibbs energy at the temperature of an experiment, corrections are added to it: zero-point vibrations and the thermal motion of the molecule — translational, rotational and vibrational. Here these corrections are computed with the same formulas that programs use, for ready-made molecules or from your own frequencies.

What to understand

  • The electronic energy refers to fixed nuclei; the enthalpy and Gibbs energy are obtained by adding zero-point vibrations and the thermal motion of the molecule.
  • Programs compute these corrections in the ideal-gas model: translation, rigid rotor, harmonic oscillator.
  • Most of the entropy of a gas is translational; vibrations contribute noticeably only through soft modes with frequencies of the order of kT/hc or below.
  • Zero-point vibrations are usually much larger than the thermal correction, and the entropy depends on the standard state and on the symmetry number.
Model limits: ideal gas, rigid rotor, harmonic oscillator
What is computed
Zero-point vibrations, enthalpy, entropy, heat capacity and the Gibbs energy correction of one molecule in the gas phase — from its mass, rotational constants and frequencies.
Which approximations are made
Ideal gas; classical rigid rotor (no centrifugal distortion, no separation into ortho and para forms); harmonic vibrations without anharmonicity and internal rotations; a single electronic state with no low-lying excited states. The rotation of H₂ (rotational temperature about 88 K, not small compared with T) is summed quantum-mechanically, with ortho and para hydrogen weighted 3 : 1; the nuclear-spin entropy is not counted, as in the tables.
What can be concluded
For small rigid molecules in the gas phase, the entropy is accurate to tenths of a J/(mol·K) with experimental frequencies. For soft modes, internal rotations and solutions it is only an estimate; the accuracy of the final G is set mainly by the electronic energy and frequencies from the calculation.

Corrections to the electronic energy

Gibbs energy of the molecule: G = Eel + Gcorr, enthalpy: H = Eel + Hcorr. In program output files these corrections are called “Thermal correction to Enthalpy / Gibbs Free Energy” (Gaussian) or “Thermal Enthalpy correction” and “G-E(el)” (ORCA). The ORCA lines “Total Enthalpy” and “Final Gibbs free energy” are already the full H and G, with Eel included: do not add it a second time.

Formulas

translation (Sackur–Tetrode)
Strans = R [ln((2πmkT/h²)3/2·kT/p) + 5/2],   Htrans = 5/2 RT (including pV = RT)
rotation (rigid rotor, high T)
linear: qrot = T/(σΘ), S = R(ln q + 1); nonlinear: qrot = √π/σ·(T³/ΘAΘBΘC)1/2, S = R(ln q + 3/2)
vibrations (harmonic oscillator), u = hcν/kT
ZPE = Σ ½hcν;   S = R Σ [u/(eu − 1) − ln(1 − e−u)]
electronic contribution and total
Sel = R ln(2S + 1);   Gcorr = ZPE + Htherm − TS

Check your knowledge

Exercises on the page: do the action on the page and choose an answer — it is checked right away.

Water at 298.15 K: what fraction of the entropy comes from translation, and why do vibrations contribute almost nothing?

Translational fraction, %

About 77 %: 144.9 of 188.7 J/(mol·K); rotation gives 43.7, vibrations 0.03. The softest vibration of water, 1595 cm⁻¹, corresponds to a temperature hcν/k ≈ 2300 K, so at 298 K its excited levels are almost unpopulated.

Why does O₂ have an electronic contribution to the entropy while N₂ does not?

The ground state of O₂ is a triplet: three spin sublevels with the same energy give R ln 3 = 9.13 J/(mol·K). N₂ is a singlet, R ln 1 = 0.

Methane, σ = 12. Click “Take the data of the selected molecule”, set σ = 1 and recalculate. By how much is the entropy overestimated?

Overestimate of the entropy, J/(mol·K)

By R ln 12 = 20.7 J/(mol·K) — half of the whole rotational contribution. At 298 K such an error shifts G by T·ΔS ≈ 6.2 kJ/mol. Programs take the symmetry number from the point group, so it is worth symmetrizing the geometry.

Compare the zero-point energy of water with the thermal correction H(298) − H(0). Which is larger, and what follows from this?

ZPE = 53.9 kJ/mol, the thermal correction is 9.9 kJ/mol, five times smaller. Zero-point vibrations exist even at 0 K, so they are always added to the electronic energy; a forgotten ZPE is a gross error in the energies of reactions that break X–H bonds.

Choose CO₂ and switch on the heat capacity plot. Which contribution grows with temperature, and why is it already noticeable for CO₂ at 298 K but not for N₂?

Vibrational: for CO₂ it is 8.0 J/(mol·K) at 298 K and 25.1 at 1000 K. CO₂ has a soft doubly degenerate bending vibration at 667 cm⁻¹ (hcν/k ≈ 960 K), while N₂ has a single vibration at 2330 cm⁻¹ (≈ 3350 K).

Switch the pressure from 1 bar to 1 atm. How much did the entropy change? And what happens with the 1 mol/L standard state?

Change in S from 1 bar to 1 atm, J/(mol·K)

By R ln(1.01325) = 0.11 J/(mol·K) — negligible. A gas at 1 mol/L and 298 K corresponds to a pressure of 24.8 bar: the entropy is lower by R ln 24.8 = 26.7 J/(mol·K), and G is higher by 7.96 kJ/mol. This correction is added when reactions in solution are computed.
