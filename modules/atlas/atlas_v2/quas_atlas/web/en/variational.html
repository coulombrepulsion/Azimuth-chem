Variational method and perturbation theory

The Schrödinger equation is solved exactly only for a few systems, so quantum chemistry relies on two approximate approaches. The variational method gives an energy that is never below the exact one: E[φ] = ⟨φ|Ĥ|φ⟩⟨φ|φ⟩ ≥ E₀. The Hartree–Fock method and CI are built on it. Perturbation theory expands the energy in a series in a small perturbation; the MP2 method is based on it.

What to understand

  • Variational principle: the energy of any trial function is not below the exact ground-state energy, so of two functions the one with the lower energy is better by this criterion. It does not follow that its density, dipole moment or behaviour near the nucleus is more accurate.
  • The linear variational method (the secular equation) underlies Hartree–Fock–Roothaan and CI; if the new basis contains the old one and the Hamiltonian is the same, the energy can only go down. When one basis is replaced by another, or for non-variational methods (for example MP2), there is no such guarantee.
  • Perturbation theory expands the energy by orders: E(1) is the average of the perturbation, and E(2) for the ground state is negative.
  • The perturbation series works well for a small perturbation and can diverge for a large one; MP2 is its second order for correlation.
Model limits: an upper bound to the energy, not the accuracy of all properties
What is computed
Variational energies of the H atom for trial functions and Gaussian basis sets; the perturbation series for the anharmonic oscillator.
Which approximations are made
A restricted trial function or a finite basis; the perturbation series is truncated at second order.
What can be concluded
The variational energy is not below the exact one, but it guarantees nothing about the density or other properties of the function; the perturbation series may diverge.

2. The linear variational method: a basis of Gaussians

The function is sought as φ = Σ ckgk, where gk = e−αkr², and the exponents form a geometric progression αk = α₀βk. Minimisation over the coefficients reduces to the secular equation det(H − ES) = 0. Each added function (the old basis remains part of the new one) can only lower the energy, and all roots approach the exact levels from above (Hylleraas–Undheim theorem).

3. Perturbation theory: the anharmonic oscillator

Ĥ = Ĥ₀ + λV̂, where Ĥ₀ = p²/2 + x²/2 is the harmonic oscillator with levels n + ½ and the perturbation is V̂ = x⁴. The first- and second-order corrections are known in closed form; the “exact” energies come from diagonalization in a basis of 40 oscillator functions.

Can the variational energy fall below the exact ground-state energy −0.5 Eh?

No: for any admissible trial function E[φ] ≥ E₀; this is the variational principle. So of two variational calculations with the same Hamiltonian, the one with the lower energy better approximates the ground-state energy. Its density or dipole moment is not necessarily more accurate.

Section 2, green curve: how does the error change as functions are added to the basis?

It decreases but always stays positive: the energy approaches the exact value from above. The error drops below 1 kcal/mol at N = 4; after that each added function brings a smaller and smaller gain.

Anharmonic oscillator, n = 0, λ = 0.1: how well does perturbation theory work?

Well: the exact energy is 0.5591, first order gives 0.575, second order 0.5488. Both are close, second order slightly closer.

What is the first-order correction E(1) for n = 0 at λ = 0.2?

E⁽¹⁾

E(1) = λ⟨0|x⁴|0⟩ = 3λ4 = 0.15. It is simply the average of the perturbation in the unperturbed ground state.

Increase λ for n = 0. What happens to the perturbation series?

From about λ ≈ 0.16 second order already has a larger error than first: the correction E(2) ∝ −λ² grows faster than the first-order error shrinks. The series for x⁴ is asymptotic — for any λ > 0 it eventually diverges.

Trial function A gives a lower energy than function B. Which of them gives a dipole moment closer to the exact one?

It cannot be determined from these data. The variational principle constrains only the energy: a first-order error in the function gives only a second-order error in the energy, while the dipole moment is sensitive to the error of the function at first order. A function with a lower energy may describe other properties worse.
