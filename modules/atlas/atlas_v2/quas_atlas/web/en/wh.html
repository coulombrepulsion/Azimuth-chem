π orbitals and pericyclic reactions

Concerted (pericyclic) reactions proceed through a cyclic transition state, and whether they are allowed is decided by the symmetry of the frontier orbitals — the Woodward–Hoffmann rules. The page works in three steps: first we build the π orbitals of polyenes by the Hückel method, then see how the signs at the ends of the HOMO set the direction of rotation on ring closure, and finally how the frontier orbitals of two molecules overlap in a cycloaddition. Lobe color is the sign of the coefficient, size its magnitude.

What to understand

  • The Hückel method is a teaching model of π orbitals: ψk of a polyene has k − 1 nodes, and 4n + 2 π electrons in a ring give a closed shell (aromaticity).
  • The course of a pericyclic reaction is set by the symmetry of the frontier orbitals (or by the correlation diagram).
  • Electrocyclic reactions: 4n electrons — conrotatory thermally, 4n + 2 — disrotatory; the reverse under irradiation.
  • Thermal [2 + 2] cycloaddition is forbidden and photochemical allowed; [4 + 2] is thermally allowed.
  • The smaller the HOMO–LUMO gap of the reactants, the faster the reaction.

1. π orbitals of polyenes: the Hückel method

Each C atom of a conjugated system contributes one p orbital; α is the energy of an electron in the atomic p orbital, β < 0 is the interaction of neighbouring atoms, and everything else is dropped. This gives levels E = α + xβ (the larger x, the lower the level) and the signs of the coefficients — which is exactly what the Woodward–Hoffmann rules need. Today the simple Hückel method is a teaching model: in quantum chemistry programs a similar scheme (the extended Hückel method) is used at most as an initial guess for SCF. 2. Electrocyclic reactions

The ends of the polyene rotate and close into a σ bond. Step 1: find the frontier orbital — on heating it is the HOMO, under irradiation the former LUMO that the electron went to. Step 2: look at the signs of its terminal coefficients. A bond forms if lobes of the same sign turn toward each other: with opposite signs at the ends this is conrotation, with equal signs disrotation.

The components approach face to face (suprafacial on both sides). New bonds form at the termini; the reaction is allowed if the frontier orbitals overlap in phase at both ends at once.

Benzene and cyclobutadiene in section 1: why is benzene aromatic and cyclobutadiene not?

In benzene 6 π electrons (4n + 2) fill three bonding levels, the shell is closed, and the delocalization energy is 2|β|. Cyclobutadiene has 4 π electrons (4n): two of them occupy, one each, a degenerate pair of nonbonding levels E = α, and the delocalization energy is 0 — this is antiaromaticity.

(E,E)-2,4-Hexadiene undergoes ring closure on heating. Which stereoisomer of 3,4-dimethylcyclobutene is formed, and why?

Trans: in the butadiene HOMO ψ₂ the signs at the termini are opposite (c₁ = 0.60, c₄ = −0.60), so bonding overlap arises only on conrotation, and conrotation puts the E,E methyls on opposite faces of the ring.

Hexatriene under heating and under irradiation: which mode is allowed in each case?

On heating, the HOMO ψ₃ with like signs at the termini decides — disrotatory. On irradiation, an electron occupies ψ₄ with opposite signs — conrotatory.

Pentadienyl cation (Nazarov reaction) and allyl cation: how do they close thermally?

Pentadienyl cation — 4 π electrons, conrotatory; allyl cation — 2 π electrons (4n + 2), disrotatory. What matters is the number of electrons, not the number of atoms.

Butadiene → cyclobutene correlation diagram: which orbital “spoils” the disrotatory path?

ψ₂ (antisymmetric with respect to σ) correlates with the unoccupied π* of cyclobutene. The ground state goes over into a doubly excited state — thermally forbidden.

Ethylene + ethylene: what changes under irradiation?

Thermally, the HOMO of one molecule and the LUMO of the other overlap in phase at only one end — [2+2] is forbidden. In excited ethylene an electron sits in the former LUMO, and its overlap with the LUMO of the second molecule is in phase at both ends — photochemically allowed.

Why does butadiene react faster with acrolein than with ethylene?

The carbonyl lowers the dienophile LUMO: the HOMO–LUMO gap is 0.97|β| versus 1.62|β| with ethylene. A smaller gap means stronger frontier-orbital interaction and a faster Diels–Alder reaction.
